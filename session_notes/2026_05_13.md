Main density quantities:

- Raw degree: number of points within the raw feature-space delta radius.
- Effective degree: number of points connected under the actual rule used by the

- Selected-point degree: same density quantities, but computed for the points
  selected by MaxHerding in each episode.

Main selection quantities:

- Selected labels per episode.
- Class-count distribution of selected points per episode.
- Entropy of selected labels per episode.
- Selected-point effective density per episode.
- Selected-point sigma distribution per episode.

Important design choice:

- Exact all-pairs density over 50k training points is expensive.
- The first diagnostic script estimates dataset-level density on a sampled set of
  anchor points, while computing selected-point density exactly against all
  training points.
- This gives a practical first look at whether selected points are unusually
  dense or sparse without launching a full new experiment.

### Selection Behavior

Selected points have higher effective degree than the dataset average.

Examples averaged over episodes:

- CIFAR10 global: selected degree about 210.9 vs dataset degree about 58.5
- CIFAR10 K=3500: selected degree about 345.3 vs dataset degree about 154.0
- CIFAR100 global: selected degree about 29.7 vs dataset degree about 11.4
- CIFAR100 K=1440: selected degree about 41.1 vs dataset degree about 19.7

This means MaxHerding is still selecting relatively dense/central points. The
local sigma setting does not automatically force sparse-region exploration.

For very small K, selected points are much denser than the dataset average in
relative terms, but the absolute degrees are still tiny. For example CIFAR100
K=10 has selected effective degree about 1.5. That is not meaningful coverage;
it is a fragmented graph.

Selected effective degree usually decreases over episodes. This suggests that
MaxHerding first selects very central/high-coverage points and later moves to
less central regions after the easiest coverage has already been taken.

### Sigma Behavior

As K increases:

- sigma mean increases,
- sigma standard deviation decreases,
- sigma coefficient of variation decreases.

Therefore, large K reduces locality. Local sigmas become more similar to each
other, which makes the kernel behave more like a global sigma kernel.

For small K, selected points tend to have lower sigma than the dataset average.
Lower sigma corresponds to locally dense regions. This is important: small K is
not pushing the algorithm toward sparse regions. It tends to select dense
micro-neighborhoods while the graph as a whole is too disconnected.

## Current Conclusion

The current self-tuning local sigma mechanism does not yet solve the desired
dense-vs-sparse selection balance.

The dense-vs-sparse goal is:

- pick enough dense-region points because they represent many samples,
- avoid wasting budget repeatedly inside the same dense region,
- still cover sparse or rare regions that may be meaningful.

What we currently see:

- Small K is too aggressive and fragments the graph.
- Large K preserves connectivity but becomes close to global sigma.
- The useful range is medium/large K, where effective graph density is comparable
  to global sigma but still slightly perturbed by local scaling.
- Any improvements from local sigma are modest and not strong evidence that
  localization alone is balancing dense and sparse regions.

## Narrowed Research Directions

We want to continue in a way that stays close to the original localization idea,
instead of drifting immediately to unrelated selection heuristics.

The two most direct next directions are:

1. Choose K using effective graph density.
2. Normalize the kernel by density.

Other ideas, such as explicit density-bin batch constraints, sparse-region
bonuses, or cluster-balanced selection, remain possible later but are less direct
extensions of the current localization work.

### Direction 1: Choose K by Effective Graph Density

The idea:

- K itself may not be the meaningful control variable.
- Effective graph density may be the meaningful control variable.
- Choose K so the resulting local-sigma graph has a desired average effective
  degree.

Naive version:

- Choose K so mean effective degree matches the global sigma graph.

Concern:

- This may collapse too strongly toward global sigma.
- If the only target is global mean degree, we may simply recover global-like
  behavior and lose the localization effect.

More useful versions to consider:

- Match global mean degree but keep local sigma variation high enough.
- Target a range around global density, not exactly global density.
- Choose K by a combined criterion:
  - effective degree is not too low,
  - graph is not too dense,
  - sigma coefficient of variation remains above some minimum.
- Choose K dynamically by episode or labeled budget:
  - early episodes may prefer denser coverage,
  - later episodes may allow more locality or sparse-region pressure.

Concrete diagnostic target:

- For CIFAR10, global effective degree is about 58.5. K=1440 is close.
- For CIFAR100, global effective degree is about 11.4. K=480 to K=960 brackets
  this region.

Question to test:

- Is performance best near a target effective degree, or near a target
  combination of degree and sigma variation?

### Direction 2: Density-Normalized Kernel

The idea:

- Local sigma changes how similarity is computed.
- But it does not explicitly reduce redundant coverage from dense regions.
- We can keep the localization/kernel direction while directly correcting for
  density dominance.

Potential normalized kernel:

```text
K_norm(i,j) = K(i,j) / sqrt(degree(i)^alpha * degree(j)^alpha)
```

or row/source-only normalization:

```text
K_norm(i,j) = K(i,j) / degree(i)^alpha
```

or target-only normalization:

```text
K_norm(i,j) = K(i,j) / degree(j)^alpha
```

Where:

- alpha = 0 means current behavior.
- alpha > 0 penalizes dense regions more strongly.

Why this stays close to localization:

- We still use the same kernel graph.
- We still use the same local/global sigma machinery.
- We only adjust the strength of edges by a density term.
- This directly targets the problem that dense regions dominate coverage.

Important design choice:

- The density used for normalization should come from the same effective graph
  used by the run, not a separate unrelated metric.
- For cutoff runs, degree can be computed from the sparse kernel graph.

Risks:

- Too much normalization may over-select outliers.
- Normalizing both source and target degrees may change the meaning of MaxHerding
  more aggressively than source-only or target-only normalization.
- We should start with a small alpha sweep, for example alpha in {0.25, 0.5, 1.0}.

### Add Density-Normalized Kernel

Potential config/CLI additions:

- `--density_normalize_kernel`
- `--density_norm_alpha`
- `--density_norm_mode`, choices:
  - `source`
  - `target`
  - `symmetric`
- `--density_norm_degree`, choices:
  - `effective`
  - `weighted`

Implementation sketch:

1. Build the kernel matrix as today.
2. Compute degree vector from the matrix:
   - effective degree: number of nonzero neighbors,
   - weighted degree: sum of kernel weights.
3. Apply normalization to nonzero entries:
   - source mode divides each row by degree_i^alpha,
   - target mode divides each column by degree_j^alpha,
   - symmetric mode divides by sqrt(degree_i^alpha * degree_j^alpha).
4. Continue MaxHerding selection with the normalized K matrix.
5. Save degree-normalization stats in the experiment directory.

Practical first version:

- Start with sparse matrices only, since our cutoff experiments use `--sparse_K`.
- Start with effective degree and alpha sweep.
- Use the same budgets and seeds as the validated MaxHerding 1NN setup.

Expected behavior:

- Source normalization should reduce the advantage of candidate points that cover
  extremely dense neighborhoods.
- Target normalization should reduce the repeated reward from already dense
  target regions.
- Symmetric normalization is more aggressive and may be closest to graph
  normalization used in spectral methods.

First experiment idea:

- MaxHerding 1NN, cutoff, same delta as before.
- Compare:
  - global sigma baseline,
  - best local K from previous results,
  - density-normalized local K with alpha 0.25, 0.5, 1.0,
  - maybe density-normalized global as a control.

Main metrics:

- accuracy curve averaged across seeds,
- final accuracy averaged across seeds,
- effective degree,
- selected effective degree,
- selected sigma,
- selected class entropy,
- overlap with non-normalized MaxHerding selections.

Implementation status:

- Added CLI/config support for density-normalized kernels.
- Added opt-in normalization after `K_general` is built.
- Defaults preserve existing behavior unless `--density_normalize_kernel` is
  passed.
- Added stats files per experiment:
  - `density_normalization_stats.json`
  - `density_normalization_stats.txt`

Available flags:

```text
--density_normalize_kernel
--density_norm_alpha <float>
--density_norm_mode source|target|symmetric
--density_norm_degree effective|weighted
--density_norm_min_degree <float>
```

Current implementation details:

- `effective` degree counts nonzero graph neighbors, excluding self.
- `weighted` degree sums kernel weights, excluding self.
- `density_norm_min_degree` floors the degree before normalization to avoid
  exploding weights for isolated points.
- `source` normalization penalizes candidate/source rows from dense regions.
- `target` normalization discounts coverage credit assigned to dense target
  regions.
- `symmetric` normalization applies both sides and is the most graph-normalized
  version.

Suggested first experiment:

- Use MaxHerding 1NN cutoff.
- Use the previous best/local-relevant K values:
  - CIFAR10: K=1440, 3500, maybe 5000
  - CIFAR100: K=480, 960, 1440, maybe 2000
- Try `density_norm_mode=symmetric` first.
- Sweep `density_norm_alpha` over 0.25, 0.5, 1.0.
- Keep global and non-normalized local runs as references.

Reasoning:

- Alpha 0 is the old method.
- Alpha 0.25 is a mild density correction.
- Alpha 0.5 is a moderate correction.
- Alpha 1.0 is aggressive and may over-select sparse/outlier regions.

### Per-Budget Behavior

The per-budget view is more nuanced than final accuracy alone.

CIFAR10:

- Global sigma benefits from normalization across early, mid, and late budgets.
- Global alpha=0.5 gives positive average gains in all phases:
  - early: about +1.74
  - mid: about +1.64
  - late: about +1.17
- Local K=3500 only benefits mildly at alpha=0.25.
- Local K=3500 with alpha=0.5 hurts especially in later budgets.

CIFAR100:

- Normalization helps early budgets but hurts later budgets.
- Global alpha=0.5 gives early gains around +1.27 but late losses around -0.16.
- Local K=1440 alpha=0.5 gives early gains around +1.47 to +1.51, but late
  losses around -0.50 to -0.87.

Interpretation:

- Density normalization may be useful when the labeled set is tiny because it
  forces broader exploration earlier.
- Later, the same correction may over-penalize dense regions that are still
  useful for classification, especially in CIFAR100.
- This suggests alpha should perhaps decrease over the AL run, or be used only
  in early episodes.

### Normalization Strength

The normalization changes are not tiny.

Examples:

- CIFAR10 local K=3500 effective:
  - mean value before: about 0.634
  - after alpha=0.25: about 0.164
  - after alpha=0.5: about 0.044
- CIFAR100 local K=1440 effective:
  - mean value before: about 0.738
  - after alpha=0.25: about 0.285
  - after alpha=0.5: about 0.127

So alpha=0.5 is already a strong intervention. Larger alpha should not be
applied broadly before we test more carefully.

### Updated Interpretation

Density normalization has a real effect.

It is not uniformly good:

- promising for CIFAR10 global,
- mildly useful for CIFAR10 local K only at small alpha,
- useful for early-budget CIFAR100,
- harmful for final-budget CIFAR100.

This is evidence that dense-region penalization can change the behavior, but it
needs budget-aware tuning. A fixed alpha may be too crude.

### Next Ideas

Better next experiments than simply increasing alpha everywhere:

- CIFAR10 global: try larger alpha values, such as 0.75 and 1.0, because alpha
  0.5 still helped final accuracy.
- CIFAR10 local K=3500: try smaller values around the useful region, such as
  0.1, 0.25, 0.35.
- CIFAR100: try weaker values, such as 0.05, 0.1, 0.25.
- Consider alpha schedules:
  - stronger alpha early,
  - weaker alpha later,
  - or alpha turned off after a few episodes.

Important direction:

- The effect may depend on label budget.
- We should not judge density normalization only by final accuracy.
- For low-budget active learning, early-budget gains may be scientifically
  important even when final accuracy does not improve.

## Plan for Next Session

The main question is now:

Why does density normalization help early budgets but sometimes hurt later?

Working hypothesis:

- Vanilla MaxHerding may already select dense/central points early.
- Density normalization may help early by preventing over-concentration in the
  densest modes and spreading selection across more representative regions.
- Later, vanilla MaxHerding naturally moves to less-central points. Applying the
  same density penalty later may push too far toward sparse or less useful
  points.

Recommended next experiments:

1. Replicate the current pilot with more seeds.
   - Keep the same reduced grid.
   - Goal: check whether the early-gain / late-loss pattern is stable.

2. Test smaller alpha values.
   - CIFAR10 local K=3500: alpha 0.1, 0.25, 0.35
   - CIFAR100 global and local K=1440: alpha 0.05, 0.1, 0.25
   - Goal: find whether CIFAR100 benefits from weaker normalization.

3. Test larger alpha only for CIFAR10 global.
   - alpha 0.5, 0.75, 1.0
   - Goal: check whether CIFAR10 global keeps improving or eventually turns over.

4. Implement an alpha schedule if the pattern remains stable.
   - Example schedule: alpha starts at 0.5 and decays to 0.
   - Simpler first schedule: alpha=0.5 for first 3 episodes, alpha=0.25 for
     middle episodes, alpha=0 for late episodes.
   - Goal: use normalization for early exploration but avoid hurting final
     exploitation.

5. Add selection diagnostics for density-normalized runs.
   - Compare selected effective degree, selected sigma, class entropy, and overlap
     with alpha=0 selections.
   - Goal: verify whether normalization is actually selecting less dense or more
     diverse points.

Discussion point for professor:

- Our original intuition was that early budgets should focus more on dense
  representative regions and later budgets should explore sparse regions.
- The pilot suggests the opposite for fixed alpha: density penalization helps
  early and hurts later.
- A plausible explanation is that MaxHerding already prioritizes dense regions
  early, and normalization helps avoid redundant dense-mode selection. Later,
  normalization may overcorrect.
